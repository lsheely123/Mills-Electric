At a Glance

Mills Electric is a licensed electrical contractor. Everything here should read as capable, safe, and squared‑away — a crew that shows up on time and leaves the panel cleaner than they found it.

We are
We are not
Direct, plain-spoken, confident
Salesy, jargon-heavy, gimmicky
Bold, structural, high-contrast
Soft, pastel, decorative, gradient-heavy
Industrial precision
Startup-playful or luxury-elegant
The mix: 65 / 25 / 8 / 2. Roughly 65% navy and neutrals, 25% white, 8% Volt Yellow, 2% Signal Red. Navy carries the page. Yellow accents and never dominates. Red is a hint — a hairline, an edge, one badge — and 2% is a ceiling, not a target. If a layout looks yellow-heavy or red-heavy, it is.

Logo

12 files · PNG · v2.0

The 2.0 artwork. Two components: the mark (the interlocked ME monogram split by the bolt) and the wordmark. Together they form the primary lockup. The mark stands alone; the wordmark does not.

What changed in 2.0. The bolt is now Signal Red, not yellow, and INC is set in red beneath the wordmark. Red entered the identity from Charlie’s business cards — it is what separates Mills Electric from a field of blue-and-yellow electrical contractors. Every v1 file is superseded; see logos/_v1/ if you need to identify old collateral.

Badge lockups

Two enclosed forms for places that need a self-contained shape. Use them where the logo sits on something busy or physical — not on the website, where the primary lockup always wins.

me-badge-circle.pngStickers, stamps, embroidered caps, social profile crops. The red arc is half the circle — never complete it in navy.
me-badge-hex.pngPatches, hard hat stickers, vehicle doors, yard signs. Carries its own cream field — do not place it on a colored panel.
Badges are physical-first. They carry more detail than the primary lockup and fall apart below about 1 inch. If it is going on a screen, reach for the primary or horizontal lockup instead.

One-color production

Three-color is always preferred. Drop to mono only when the production method genuinely can’t do three — embroidery, single-plate print, laser etch, hot foil.

me-mono-navy.pngOne-color print, etched metal, invoices.
me-mono-white.pngKnockout on photos, dark shirts, vinyl.
me-mono-yellow.pngNavy shirts, safety vests, night-visible signage.
me-mono-red.pngRare. Emergency-service collateral and one-color red stamps only.
Mono red is not a shortcut to “on-brand.” An all-red lockup reads as an alarm, not as Mills Electric. It exists for single-plate emergency collateral. If you are reaching for it on a website or a shirt, you want mono navy or mono white.

Clear space & minimum size

X = the cap-height of the M in the wordmark. Reserve X on all four sides. Nothing enters that zone — no text, no rules, no photo edges, no other logos.

Also: don’t rebuild the wordmark in another typeface (it’s fixed artwork); don’t separate the bolt to use as a standalone icon; don’t revert the bolt to yellow to match old collateral; and don’t add red to the MILLS ELECTRIC wordmark itself — red belongs to the bolt and to INC, nowhere else inside the lockup. Pantone values are nearest-neighbour matches. Confirm against a physical book before any large print run. The raw red in the 2.0 artwork samples at #FB0107; the token is set to #E4151B because that is the nearest value that clears AA in both directions against white — see the contrast table below.

Red is trim, not a theme. Charlie put red on the business cards to stand out in a trade full of blue-and-yellow vans, and that is exactly the job it keeps: a hairline under a heading, the sheared edge of a hero, the divider in the horizontal lockup, one emergency button. Its budget is 2% of the visual field. Red never fills a section background, never sets a headline, and never carries body copy.

Brand red and danger red are different colors on purpose. Signal Red #E4151B means “Mills Electric”; danger #A4161A means “something is wrong.” They are three full steps apart in value, so they read as distinct even side by side. Two rules keep it honest: a status never uses the core red, and status colour never travels alone — always pair it with an icon and a word. And warning stays burnt orange, never yellow, for the same reason it always has.

zap is reserved. It echoes the logo bolt, so use it only for the company itself or top-level “electrical service” concepts — never as a generic bullet. Yellow icons are for emphasis only: a grid of twelve yellow icons is wrong. Make them navy and let one be yellow.

Photography over illustration

Real crews, real panels, real job sites. Prefer wide shots with strong horizontals and verticals — the brand is structural. Crop tight on hands-at-work and finished installs; avoid stock handshakes. Grade slightly cool to sit with the navy. For text legibility, overlay navy at 60–75% or run a navy-to-transparent gradient from the left. No stock illustration, no isometric vector scenes, no 3D renders.

Buttons

The only red button in the system. White label, never navy.
On navy surfaces, the button goes white.

48px tall (40 compact), 24px side padding, 6px radius, Inter 600. Focus ring is a 3px yellow halo on every variant. Primary is the yellow one, and there is exactly one per screen section. The red emergency variant is capped at one per site — it is the 24/7 call button and nothing else. Give it a white label (4.75:1); a navy label on red is 3.30:1 and fails. If a red button ever sits next to a yellow one, put navy or white between them.

The bolt accent

The logo’s bolt can inform a diagonal accent — an angled section divider or a sheared edge on a hero image. Three rules: the angle is always 12°, matching the bolt’s dominant slope; one diagonal per page; and never as a background texture, watermark, or repeating motif. It’s a signature, not a pattern.

Red trim

Signal Red earns its keep as a line, not a fill. Four sanctioned uses, and that is the whole list:

The 2px rule A 2px red hairline under a section heading or above a footer. Token: --me-trim-red.
The sheared edge The 12° diagonal on a hero, drawn in red instead of yellow. One per page — pick red or yellow, never both.
The emergency badge A red-100 band with navy type for the 24/7 line, or the single red button.
The lockup itself The bolt, INC, and the horizontal divider bar. Fixed artwork — never recolored.

Anything beyond these four is a new decision, not an application of the system. Red section backgrounds, red headlines, red body copy, red icon sets, and red-on-yellow pairings are all out.

07

Voice

Plain English. Lead with the outcome, then the credential. Read it out loud — if you run out of breath, cut it.

Write this
Not this
“We’ll replace your panel.”
“We facilitate electrical infrastructure modernization.”
“Same-day service in Kent County.”
“Fast, reliable service you can trust!”
“Lights back on today. Licensed and insured.”
“Don’t let faulty wiring burn your house down.”
Active voice, short sentences.
Passive constructions and stacked clauses.

Say licensed, bonded, insured exactly once per page — it’s a fact, not a slogan. No exclamation points in headlines. No fear-based safety copy: state the risk factually and offer the fix.

Naming. The legal name is Mills Electric Inc — no comma before Inc. On second reference, Mills Electric. Never “Mills Electrical” or “ME Inc” in customer-facing copy.
