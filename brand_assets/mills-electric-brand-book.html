At a Glance

Mills Electric is a licensed electrical contractor. Everything here should read as capable, safe, and squared‑away — a crew that shows up on time and leaves the panel cleaner than they found it.

The mix: 65 / 25 / 8 / 2. Roughly 65% black and neutrals, 25% white, 8% Brushed Gold, 2% Signal Red. Black carries the page. Gold accents and never dominates. Red is a hint — a hairline, an edge, one badge — and 2% is a ceiling, not a target. Gold is the one that will tempt you. A metallic reads as expensive, so the instinct is to spend more of it; at 8% it signals quality, at 20% it signals a payday-loan storefront.

Logo

v3.1 · 10 files · PNG · rebuilt 3×

The 3.1 artwork. Two components: the mark (the interlocked ME monogram split by the bolt) and the wordmark. Together they form the primary lockup. The mark stands alone; the wordmark does not.

What changed in 3.1 — the red is flat. Through 3.0 the bolt, INC, and the divider bar carried a gradient running roughly #8B0000 to #FE493A. All of it is now the single flat value #E90206, measured off the new artwork. Nothing else about the red moved: same shapes, same placement, same 2% budget. The monogram and wordmark remain Graphite Black; the E-bars remain Brushed Gold with the sheen baked in. Superseded sets: logos/_v3.0/ (gradient red), logos/_v2/ (navy and yellow), logos/_v1/.
Provenance — read before sending anything to print. The 3.1 board arrived, like 3.0 before it, as a flattened RGB image with no alpha channel: the transparency you see was a drawn checkerboard, and the files carry visible resampling artifacts. Everything here was cut from that board and keyed back to real transparency. That is fine for screen and adequate for small print, but these are not master files. The board put the primary at just 790 px wide, with JPEG-soft, wavy edges. The shipped files are rebuilt at 3× — the primary is now 2370×1371 — by re-deriving each colour region's contour at high resolution and re-rendering it. Shape, colour and proportion are unchanged (silhouette match 0.999, identical bounding box and aspect ratio); what improved is edge definition. Be clear about what that is and is not. It removes compression mush and gives clean, straight, smooth contours, so the lockups hold up far better when scaled. It does not recover detail the board never had — the gold's brushed texture is still 790 px of information stretched over 2370. For a vehicle wrap or a yard sign, still get vector (SVG, AI, or EPS) from the designer. Marked derived below: files not supplied, reconstructed here.
Two things changed in 3.1 besides the red. Worth knowing, because neither was asked for. One: the circle badge was redesigned. In 3.0 its ring was split — half black, half red, crossing behind the monogram. In 3.1 it is a single unbroken red ring with the black arc gone. That is a different badge, not a recolor. Two: the board itself is lower resolution. 3.1 is a 3-up where 3.0 supplied a 2-up, so the primary arrived at 790 px wide against 3.0's 1252 px, and the proportions shifted with it (see the ratio note below). The rebuild described above more than compensates on edge quality, but it cannot add back source detail. If either change was unintended, the 3.0 files are still in logos/_v3.0/.

One-color production

Not supplied in 3.0 — do not improvise these. The 2.0 set had four one-color lockups (me-mono-navy, -white, -yellow, -red). No 3.0 equivalents were delivered, and the black-and-gold artwork cannot be flattened to one ink by hand without losing the E-bars, which are the only thing separating the monogram from a plain ME. Requests to make: me-mono-black (one-color print, etched metal, invoices), me-mono-white (knockout on photos and dark shirts), me-mono-gold (black shirts, night-visible signage), and me-mono-red (emergency collateral only). Until they exist, a one-color job uses the full-color lockup or waits.

Pantone values are nearest-neighbour matches. Confirm against a physical book before any large print run. Graphite Black is a rich black, not 100K — flat 100K prints muddy and grey across a large solid, which is most of this brand. Signal Red is now flat. In 3.0 the bolt carried a gradient that ran from #8B0000 to #FE493A; the 3.1 artwork replaced it with a single value, measured at #E90206, and the token is set to exactly that. It clears AA in both directions against white at 4.69:1.

Why black and gold, and what it costs. Navy-and-yellow is the uniform of the electrical trade — it is what the vans, the invoices, and the competitors all look like. Black and gold is a deliberate step out of that lineup: it reads as finish work and licensed craft rather than as a truck that shows up. The trade-off is real and worth naming. Volt Yellow was a warning color, legible at fifty feet on a vest. Brushed Gold is not; it is a quality color. So gold never carries a safety message. Anything that has to be seen before it is read — hazard tape, vests, lockout tags, the emergency line — uses Signal Red or high-vis safety yellow as equipment, outside the brand palette. Do not press gold into that job.

The brushed finish

“Brushed” is a finish, not a second hex. Two rules, and the split between them matters. The lockup carries its own sheen — the E-bars are fixed artwork and the gradient is baked in; never flatten them. Everything you build is flat — buttons, rules, icons, bands and type all use the flat token #DFAD37, no gradient, no bevel, no shine. The sheen below is the artwork’s own, reproduced for foil stamping, vehicle lettering and signage, where real light hits real material and a flat fill looks dead. If you are filling a rectangle in CSS, you want the flat token.

The artwork sheen — measured, large format onlySampled from the delivered E-bars: #A37216 → #DFAD37 → #FEE770, with fine striation and a single highlight. The axis runs along the long edge of the letterform, never diagonally across a lockup. Print: PMS 872 C metallic ink or a matte gold foil. Vinyl: brushed-gold cast film.
Not this — the “chrome gold” gradientMultiple white blowouts, a diagonal axis, and darks pushed to olive. Brushed metal has one highlight and never hits pure white. This reads as a trophy or a casino. Never on screen, never on a lockup, never as a text fill.

The stops above are measured from the delivered artwork, not invented: sampling the E-bars gives a range of #A37216 at the darkest to #FEE770 at the brightest, with a median of #DFAD37. Stay inside that range, keep the axis along the long edge, and allow exactly one highlight. Go brighter, darker, or add a second blowout and it stops matching the lockup sitting next to it. Embroidery on black shirts is the one place a flat thread beats a sheen — use old gold thread (Madeira 1070 or equivalent) and skip the gradient entirely.

Red ramp

3.1 flattened the red and the whole ramp moved with it. Through 3.0 the bolt was a gradient running roughly #8B0000 to #FE493A; it is now the single measured value #E90206, and every step below is rebuilt on that hue. Against white the core is AA in both directions at 4.69:1. On Graphite Black it reaches 4.19:1 — up from 3.30:1 on the old navy, but still short of the 4.5:1 body-text bar, so the rule holds: red-300 for red type on black. The 2% budget applies regardless.

Red is trim, not a theme. Charlie put red on the business cards to stand out in a trade full of blue-and-yellow vans, and that is exactly the job it keeps: a hairline under a heading, the sheared edge of a hero, the divider in the horizontal lockup, one emergency button. Its budget is 2% of the visual field. Red never fills a section background, never sets a headline, and never carries body copy. Red got harder in 3.0, not easier. Gold sits much closer to red in value than yellow did, so red-on-gold collapsed from 3.09:1 to 2.27:1 — see the contrast table. The two must never touch.

Brand red and danger red are different colors on purpose. Signal Red #E90206 means “Mills Electric”; danger #B60205 means “something is wrong.” They are three full steps apart in value, so they read as distinct even side by side. Two rules keep it honest: a status never uses the core red, and status colour never travels alone — always pair it with an icon and a word. And warning stays burnt orange — which now matters more than it did, because #B54708 and Brushed Gold are close enough in hue that a gold-tinted warning would be unreadable as a status at all.

The three rules people break. One. Never white text on Brushed Gold and never gold text on white — both land at 2.06:1. The one thing that got worse is red-against-gold, and the fix is the rule above: keep them apart.

Photography over illustration

Real crews, real panels, real job sites. Prefer wide shots with strong horizontals and verticals — the brand is structural. Crop tight on hands-at-work and finished installs; avoid stock handshakes. Grade neutral and contrasty — 2.0 called for a cool grade to sit with the navy, and that is now wrong: a cool grade over Graphite Black turns the blacks blue and the gold green. No stock illustration, no isometric vector scenes, no 3D renders.

Buttons

The only red button in the system. White label, never black.
On black surfaces, the button goes white.

48px tall (40 compact), 24px side padding, 6px radius, Inter 600. Primary is the gold one, and there is exactly one per screen section — its label is black, at 9.53:1. The focus ring is a 3px gold-600 halo on light surfaces and gold-300 on dark; core gold is only 2.06:1 on white and fails the 3:1 bar a focus indicator has to clear on its own. The red emergency variant is capped at one per site — it is the 24/7 call button and nothing else. Give it a white label (4.69:1); a black label on red is 4.19:1 and fails. A red button must never sit beside a gold one — at 2.27:1 they read as one smear. Put black or white between them, or separate them entirely.

The bolt accent

The logo’s bolt can inform a diagonal accent — an angled section divider or a sheared edge on a hero image. Three rules: the angle is always 12°, matching the bolt’s dominant slope; one diagonal per page; and never as a background texture, watermark, or repeating motif. It’s a signature, not a pattern.

Red trim

Signal Red earns its keep as a line, not a fill. Four sanctioned uses, and that is the whole list:

The 2px rule A 2px red hairline under a section heading or above a footer. Token: --me-trim-red.
The sheared edge The 12° diagonal on a hero, drawn in red instead of gold. One per page — pick red or gold, never both, and never let the two edges meet.
The emergency badge A red-100 band with black type (12.10:1) for the 24/7 line, or the single red button.
The lockup itself The bolt, INC, and the horizontal divider bar. Fixed artwork — never recolored.

Anything beyond these four is a new decision, not an application of the system. Red section backgrounds, red headlines, red body copy, red icon sets, and red-on-gold pairings are all out.

Voice

Plain English. Lead with the outcome, then the credential. Read it out loud — if you run out of breath, cut it.

Write this
Not this
“We’ll replace your panel.”
“We facilitate electrical infrastructure modernization.”
“Same-day service in Kent County.”
“Fast, reliable service you can trust!”
“Lights back on today. Licensed and insured.”
“Don’t let faulty wiring burn your house down.”
Active voice, short sentences.
Passive constructions and stacked clauses.

Say licensed, bonded, insured exactly once per page — it’s a fact, not a slogan. No exclamation points in headlines. No fear-based safety copy: state the risk factually and offer the fix.

Naming. The legal name is Mills Electric Inc — no comma before Inc. On second reference, Mills Electric. Never “Mills Electrical” or “ME Inc” in customer-facing copy.
